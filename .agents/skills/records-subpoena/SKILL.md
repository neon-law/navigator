# Scoping a records subpoena

A subpoena that stops too early fails without anyone noticing. The custodian swears "no records," the affidavit is true
for the window we asked about, and the event we needed happened a month after it. Because a second subpoena often comes
after the discovery cutoff, it may need leave of court. Get the window right the first time.

## The rule: widest defensible window

Each records category gets the widest time frame we can justify in one sentence. Narrow the **subject** (which account,
which item, name only) to manage privacy and burden. Do not narrow the **time** to do it.

## Steps

1. **List the anchor events, with dates.** These are the instruments, transactions, recordings, and communications
   the subpoena is about. Cite the document in the file that dates each one.
2. **Set each start date** at the earliest point a responsive record could exist, such as an account opening, an
   application, or the first contact. Do not start at the date of the event we care about.
3. **Set each end date** at the latest point a responsive record could exist. Do not end at the date we expect.
   Use the tails below.
4. **Give an item-specific request no end date.** A request for one check number, wire reference, or file number is
   already narrow, so ask for it "from [issue date] through the date of production."
5. **Write a scope table into the draft.** Give one row per category, with its start, its end, and the reason for
   each. A bound with no written reason is an error.
6. **Test both edges.** For each category, ask: "If the event happened one day after our end date, or one day before
   our start, would we miss it, and would a 'no records' affidavit mislead us?" Fix any yes.
7. **Confirm the custodian holds the account.** Before relying on a "no records" answer, make sure the item was
   drawn on, deposited at, or held by this custodian. A document in the file must tie the account to the custodian.
   Otherwise the answer proves nothing. If nothing ties them, add a request that identifies the account, or subpoena the
   other institution too.

## Tails: how late a record can exist

- **A check, either the drawer's paid item or the payee's deposit**: Through the date of production. A bank may still
  pay a check more than six months old (Cal. Com. Code § 4404), so six months is a floor, not a ceiling.
- **Account statements around a disputed payment**: Through the date the dispute surfaced, such as a demand, an escrow,
  or the filing, or at least 12 months after the last anchor event, whichever is later.
- **Loan funding, payoff, or reconveyance**: Through the payoff demand or the reconveyance, or the present if neither
  happened.
- **An escrow or title file**: Opening through closing or cancellation, plus every later demand, reconveyance, or claim
  on the same property.
- **Communications with a party**: Through the date of production, unless a privilege or relevance line is clearly
  drawn.

## Privacy and quash risk

Consumer records (CCP § 1985.3) invite a motion to quash. Answer that risk with these, not with a shorter window:

- tie each category to a fact in dispute, in the attachment itself;
- ask for "name only" or "records sufficient to identify" where that is enough;
- allow redaction of signature cards, tax ID numbers, and unrelated account holders;
- name accounts and items specifically.

If the lawyer chooses a narrower window anyway, record that choice and its reason in the scope table. It must read as a
decision, not as an oversight.

## Before issuing

- Read the rendered Attachment 3 against the scope table.
- Check the discovery cutoff (CCP § 2024.020) and the production-date minimums (CCP § 2020.410(c), § 1985.3(b)). A
  wider window does not change the production date.
- Note in the matter's handoff note what a "no records" answer would and would not prove.
